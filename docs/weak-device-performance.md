# Will this be fast on slow and weak devices?

*Scope: the adopted rendering concept (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md`, `docs/presenter-architecture.md`, `docs/input-latch.md`) assessed against the requirement, and the v7 tree assessed against the same requirement by reading the code. Claims are labelled: **verified** means read out of the tree at the cited lines, **derived** means arithmetic shown here from verified inputs, **needs measurement** means the number is not obtainable by reading and the exact command is given in §12.*

## 1. The target, and the two answers

"Weak device" here means three machines that differ in what they run out of: a several-year-old phone in a mobile browser (bandwidth- and thermally-bound, tile-based GPU), an integrated-GPU laptop (bandwidth-bound, sharing memory with the CPU), and a low-end Chromebook (all of the above plus a slow CPU and a slow network for the initial payload). What they have in common is that none of them is short of triangles. All three run out of fill rate, memory bandwidth, and — before any of that — patience during the download.

**The concept is well-aimed at exactly this requirement, and it is honest about its costs in outline but not in magnitude.** Frame-rate amplification and free dynamic resolution are the right two levers for weak hardware, and before the first triangle is the cheap moment to choose them. What the adopted document bounds as "~0.2–0.5 ms on weak GPUs plus 2–3 color+depth targets of memory" (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:126-127`) is a cost that scales with output resolution and is nowhere bounded by the architecture. §2 puts numbers on it.

**The implementation today pays the concept's full price on the weak target and collects none of its benefit there.** This is not a defect — stage A is defined as the identity case (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:75-77`) and the extra pass is the price of having the seam exist early. But it means that measured today on a phone, ATW-first would read as a pure regression against a renderer that drew straight to the swapchain, and that is better known in advance than discovered. §3 and §4 show why, and §5 through §8 find larger costs that have nothing to do with ATW at all.

The short version: **the two cheapest and largest weak-device levers in this codebase are one line each, neither is ATW, and neither is mentioned in either design document** — backface culling is off (`crates/ember-engine/src/renderer.rs:963`) and the render resolution is pinned to the device pixel ratio with no cap (`crates/ember-engine/src/app.rs:233-234`, `renderer.rs:278`).

## 2. What the concept promises weak devices, and what it charges them

The promise is stated as the main lever for the requirement: a weak client renders the scene at 30–40 Hz and the warp fills to display rate, with dynamic resolution on the SceneFrame free because the warp resamples anyway (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:62-65`, restated at `:118-119`). That is the right shape of answer. Decoupling scene cost from present cadence is the only way a device that cannot render 60 complete frames per second still feels responsive, and mouse-look is the signal players judge responsiveness by. Nothing below disputes the promise; §2 is about its price.

**The always-on pass is a bandwidth cost, and bandwidth is the weak device's scarce resource.** Every displayed frame reads the full scene color texture and writes the full surface. The scene color is `Rgba8UnormSrgb` (`renderer.rs:144`) and the surface is a 4-bytes-per-pixel format, so the pass moves 8 bytes per output pixel that a direct-to-swapchain renderer would not move. Derived, at 60 displayed frames per second:

| Surface | Pixels | Per frame | Per second |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1280×720 | 921,600 | 7.4 MB | 442 MB/s |
| 1920×1080 | 2,073,600 | 16.6 MB | 995 MB/s |
| 2560×1440 | 3,686,400 | 29.5 MB | 1.77 GB/s |

An identity blit is a perfectly coherent read, so these figures are close to real traffic rather than a worst case. Against an integrated GPU sharing roughly 25–50 GB/s with the CPU, the 1440p row is 4–7% of total system bandwidth spent copying an image to itself. Against a phone with a tenth of that bandwidth, the same row is not affordable at all — which is an argument for capping resolution (§6), not against the architecture, but it is an argument the adopted document does not make.

The framing "~0.2–0.5 ms on weak GPUs" (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:126`) is defensible at 720p and optimistic by roughly a factor of four at the resolution the shipped page actually requests on a high-DPI display (§6). A cost quoted as a fixed millisecond figure invites the reader to treat it as fixed; it scales with output pixels, and the output pixel count is currently unbounded.

**The second render target is memory, and the ring multiplies it.** Scene color at 4 bytes per pixel plus depth at 4 bytes per pixel (`renderer.rs:142`, `:144`) is 8 bytes per pixel per SceneFrame. Derived: 7.4 MB at 1280×720, 29.5 MB at 2560×1440. The design calls for a ring of 2–3 frames (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:53-54`), which at 1440p is 59–88 MB of render targets before the swapchain, on devices where a browser tab's whole GPU allocation may be a few hundred megabytes. The ring is correct for the architecture and it is also the point where "2–3 color+depth targets of memory" stops being a rounding error. Decoupling scene resolution from surface resolution (invariant I3, `docs/presenter-architecture.md:79`) is what makes this affordable — and it is exactly the mechanism that is present and unused today.

**The guard band is overdraw, and it is charged before it is used.** Stage B widens the rendered FOV by about 7° so edge reveal does not show black (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:79-81`). Derived: widening a 70° vertical FOV to 77° while holding the display FOV fixed multiplies the rendered tangent extent by `tan(38.5°)/tan(35°) = 0.7954/0.7002 = 1.136` per axis, so the SceneFrame must carry `1.136² = 1.29` times the pixels of the displayed image — about 29% more scene shading, permanently, for pixels that are discarded on every frame where the player is not turning. On a fill-rate-bound device that is a larger number than the warp pass it enables. It is still likely the right trade, because the alternative to a guard band is a visible black wedge under exactly the fast turns the warp exists to serve — but the doc presents the guard band as a correctness requirement (`:105-106`) without pricing it, and 29% of scene cost is not a detail on a device that is already missing its frame budget.

**Where the trade pays, and where it does not.** The honest boundary is worth stating because neither document states it. ATW-first pays when the scene pass is the expensive thing and the display rate is high: the fixed per-displayed-frame cost of the warp buys a large reduction in perceived latency and a decoupled scene rate. It does not pay when the scene is *cheap* relative to the blit — and that is the regime this game is in today (§5: roughly 3–5 thousand vertices and no meaningful shading load). A scene that costs less than a fullscreen resample gains nothing from being decoupled from presentation and pays the resample anyway. The architecture is still the right bet, because the scene will not stay this cheap and retrofitting is what the adopted document correctly refuses to do — but "the trade pays" is a claim about a future scene, not about the current one, and that distinction is missing from `docs/atw-first-rendering.md:56-72`.

## 3. Today the price is paid and the benefit is not delivered

Stage A is an identity blit by construction, and the code implements exactly that: the present pass draws one fullscreen triangle (`renderer.rs:749`) whose fragment shader is a single `textureSample` with no transform (`crates/ember-engine/src/present.wgsl:30-31`). Verified.

The benefit stage A cannot yet deliver is the latency win, and it is unavailable for two independent structural reasons rather than a shader detail. The first is that the present pass has no pose input at all: its bind group holds the scene colour view and a sampler and nothing else (`renderer.rs:909-922`), so there is no delta for it to apply even in principle. The second is that there would be no delta to supply: `render_impl` runs the scene pass and the present pass inside one call against one `Frame` (`renderer.rs:597-801`), from one camera sampled once, and the input accumulator is drained immediately after the game's update and before either pass (`app.rs:280-281`) — which is the property `docs/input-latch.md:16` names, that anything reading input after that line reads zero, silently. So the transform stage A applies is the identity, and it stays the identity until both the latch and the pose plumbing change.

So: **on the weak target the engine currently spends the ATW pass's bandwidth every displayed frame and receives, in exchange, a bit-identical copy of an image it already had.** That is the correct state for stage A under a deliberately staged plan. It is also the state a measurement taken today would record. The finding is not that this is wrong; it is that the payback lives entirely in unlanded work, so no weak-device measurement taken before stage B can validate the architecture, and none should be cited as if it could.

## 4. The weak target has neither the instruments nor the levers

This is the sharpest concept-versus-implementation mismatch in the tree, and it is a mismatch with the adopted document's own reasoning rather than with a general principle.

The adopted document asks for debug hooks early — a scene-Hz throttle slider and a latency/timing HUD — with the stated purpose that warp artifacts are "exercised continuously instead of discovered on a slow machine" (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:120-122`). The rig exists and does what was asked: the throttle skips the scene pass while the presenter keeps re-presenting (`renderer.rs:605-612`), the overlay carries the slider and the scene-age readout (`crates/ember-engine/src/overlay.rs:82-92`), and the age is reported from the renderer (`renderer.rs:545-548`).

Every piece of it is compiled out on wasm. Verified, individually:

- the overlay module is native-only (`crates/ember-engine/src/lib.rs:10-11`);
- `set_scene_hz_cap` and `scene_age_ms` are native-only (`renderer.rs:539-548`);
- the throttle fields do not exist on wasm (`renderer.rs:196-199`);
- the scene-pass decision is hardcoded true on wasm (`renderer.rs:611-612`);
- the overlay-driving redraw branch is native-only, and the wasm branch calls plain `render` (`app.rs:282-302`).

So the rig built to keep slow-machine behaviour continuously visible runs only on the machines that are not slow, and the browser — the only place the weak targets exist — has no throttle, no scene-age readout, no frame-time readout, and no way to turn any of it on. The instrument and the machine it was built to characterise never meet.

Two secondary consequences follow. First, the scene-Hz throttle is the *mechanism* by which a weak device would hold a frame budget, not merely a debug affordance; being native-only means the production adaptive path, when it comes, has no existing hook on the target that needs it. Second, `scene_age_ms` is the one number that would tell a player's browser whether the presenter is re-presenting stale frames, and it is unavailable precisely where staleness is expected.

The observability half of this gap is worked out in detail elsewhere and should not be re-derived here: `docs/latency-observability.md` reaches the same finding from the measurement side, recording the scene-age readout as native-only and the wasm path as always rendering a scene pass (`docs/latency-observability.md:52`, `:74`), and proposes keeping metrics collection target-neutral with the snapshot exposed as JSON from the wasm API (`docs/latency-observability.md:205`). This document's interest is narrower and complementary: not that the numbers are unavailable on the web, but that the *throttle* — the mechanism a weak device would use to hold a budget — is unavailable with them.

A caveat in fairness: diagnostics as such are not lost on wasm. The workspace enables tracing's `log` feature specifically so that events fall back to `log` records when no subscriber is installed, and `console_log` prints them (`Cargo.toml:23-25`, `app.rs:319`), so the frame-stall warning (`app.rs:267-278`) does reach the browser console. The stall warning is the only weak-device telemetry that survives the target split, and it reports a symptom without any of the numbers that would explain it.

## 5. Fill rate is the budget, and two absences spend it

**Backface culling is off.** `cull_mode: None` (`renderer.rs:963`), with `front_face` left at the wgpu default of counter-clockwise via `..Default::default()` (`renderer.rs:964`). Verified.

Every mesh in the shipped arena is a closed box or a closed GLB part, so exactly half of every mesh's triangles face away from any camera. Those triangles are still transformed, still rasterized, and — where the away-facing side happens to be drawn first — still fragment-shaded before being overwritten. The built-in cube's faces are emitted in a fixed order (`renderer.rs:1058-1066`), so for roughly half of all camera orientations the far face of a given axis is rasterized before the near one, shaded, and then replaced. The fragment shader is not trivial work: a `normalize`, a `dot`, a `pow`, and an `exp` per fragment (`crates/ember-engine/src/shader.wgsl:70-76`).

Derived: enabling backface culling removes about 50% of rasterized triangles and, on the front-to-back-unlucky half of orientations, close to 50% of shaded fragments for the affected surfaces. On a fill-rate-bound device this is the single largest available win in the tree, and it is one line.

It is not entirely free, and the caveat is the reason it has to be a deliberate change rather than a reflex. The built-in cube's winding is counter-clockwise as seen from outside — verified by construction at `renderer.rs:1073-1080`, where face `+Z` emits corners `(-0.5,-0.5)`, `(0.5,-0.5)`, `(0.5,0.5)` in the plane `z = +0.5`, which is counter-clockwise viewed from `+Z`. Every arena surface is viewed from outside its own box: the floor slab from above (`crates/arena/src/online.rs:794-799`), the four wall slabs from inside the arena, which is outside each slab (`:800-812`), obstacles and player bodies likewise. So culling is safe for the shipped web game. It is *not* automatically safe for `crates/game`, whose native-only `plane_mesh` (`crates/game/src/main.rs:128`) is single-sided and would disappear when viewed from the wrong side, nor is it verified for the twelve GLB viewmodel primitives. The change is one line plus a winding audit of those two mesh sources.

**There is no frustum culling anywhere in the workspace.** Verified as an absence: a search for culling, frustum, or visibility logic across all Rust and WGSL sources returns only `renderer.rs:963`. Every obstacle, wall, pad, remote player and bullet is submitted every frame regardless of where the camera points (`online.rs:788-912`). In a first-person camera at the centre of an enclosed arena, roughly half the arena is behind the player at any moment.

The absolute cost is small today — the arithmetic below shows the scene is not vertex-bound — but the structural point is not small, and it is the clearest thing neither design document addresses. The adopted document requires that "projection and culling code must handle oversized/asymmetric frusta" (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:105-106`), which presumes a culling stage exists to be widened. It does not exist. So when stage B lands, the guard band's 29% of extra scene pixels (§2) arrives with no culling stage to widen and therefore no mechanism by which the widened frustum admits more geometry — the geometry was never being rejected in the first place. The guard band's *rasterization* cost lands in full; its *culling* correctness requirement is vacuous. That is a benign accident today and a stale requirement in the plan, and someone sizing stage B from `:105-106` will look for code that is not there.

**The scene is not vertex-bound or draw-call-bound, which is why fill rate is the whole story.** Derived, for a full eight-player match (`MAX_PLAYERS = 8`, `crates/arena-core/src/shooter.rs:71`), all alive at full health (`MAX_HP = 3`, `:69`):

- static geometry: 1 floor + 4 walls + 14 obstacles (`generate_arena` returns exactly 14, `shooter.rs:99-100`) + 4 pad slabs + up to 4 active pickups (`generate_pads` returns 4, `shooter.rs:131`) = 27 instances;
- seven remote players at 1 body + 1 head + 8 gun parts + 3 health pips each (`online.rs:872-899`) = 91 instances;
- own viewmodel at 8 gun parts + 4 arm parts + 5 muzzle flash cones (the star, since the v20 rounds pass) + the crosshair (2 hairline bars on native, 0 on the web, where the page's own `div#crosshair` stands in; the aim dot this line once counted is gone since v20) = 17 to 19 instances;
- total ≈ **135 to 137 instances**, plus bullets, and plus what v20 adds on top of this list: a remote player who is firing carries 5 flash cones for the 35 to 60 ms of `flash_ms` where v19 gave him 1 cube, and up to `feel::MARK_CAP` (96) impact-hole discs can be live on the surfaces at once, each 20 s old at most.

The gun and arm part counts come from the viewmodel GLB itself, which carries twelve mesh primitives named `slide`, `barrel`, `frame`, `grip`, `guard`, `sight_f`, `sight_r`, `strip`, `hand_r`, `arm_r`, `hand_l`, `arm_l`; the first eight are gun parts and the four hand/arm names are split off by prefix at `online.rs:50-53`. With the built-in cube that is 13 mesh entries, so at most **13 draw calls** per frame (`renderer.rs:714-719`). Roughly 64 of the instances use the 36-vertex cube (`renderer.rs:1057`), giving about 2,300 cube vertices plus a small number from the 19 KB viewmodel — on the order of **3–5 thousand vertices per displayed frame**.

Three to five thousand vertices and thirteen draw calls is nothing on any GPU made this century, while a single 1440p present pass touches 3.7 million fragments — a ratio of roughly a thousand to one. That is why every recommendation in §11 is about pixels and none is about batching.

## 6. Resolution is the one lever that exists, and it is pinned at the worst setting

The hook is present, correct, and unused — as `docs/presenter-architecture.md:79` already records for invariant I3. This section adds what it costs, which is the part not yet written down.

`scene_scale` is initialised to 1.0 (`renderer.rs:278`), read only when creating scene targets (`renderer.rs:279`, `:569`, and the scaling arithmetic at `:868-869`), and has no setter anywhere in the workspace. Verified: six occurrences total, one of them the doc comment at `renderer.rs:7`, none of them a write after construction.

What makes this the top lever rather than a nice-to-have is what the surface size actually is on the shipped page. The canvas is styled to `min(1280px, 96vw)` CSS pixels at a 16:9 aspect (`web/games/arena/v7/index.html:67-68`), and the engine sizes the backing store by multiplying the CSS size by the device pixel ratio every frame, with no cap (`app.rs:230-237`, the multiply at `:233-234`). Derived:

| Device | CSS width | DPR | Backing store | Pixels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Desktop, standard DPI | 1280 | 1 | 1280×720 | 0.92 M |
| Desktop or tablet, retina | 1280 | 2 | 2560×1440 | 3.69 M |
| Phone, 420 CSS px viewport | ~403 | 3 | ~1209×680 | 0.82 M |

The retina row is the one that matters, and it is counter-intuitive in exactly the way that makes it a real bug rather than a tuning question: a high-DPI screen is not evidence of a fast GPU. A budget Chromebook or a mid-range tablet with a 2× panel renders **four times** as many pixels as the same page on a standard-DPI desktop, and it renders them through the scene pass, the depth store, and the present pass alike. Every bandwidth figure in §2 quadruples.

`scene_scale` is precisely the knob for this, it costs nothing to use because the presenter resamples regardless (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:118-119`), and it is pinned to 1.0. Capping the *scene* resolution while leaving the surface at native DPR is strictly better than capping the canvas, because the UI stays crisp — the overlay and the page's DOM crosshair and scoreboard (`web/games/arena/v7/index.html:112-119`) are composited at surface resolution — while only the world image is resampled. That is the ATW architecture delivering its promised benefit, available today, at stage A, for the cost of a setter and a policy.

A second-order note on the same code path: the canvas size is recomputed every frame by reading `client_width()` and `client_height()` (`app.rs:233-234`), which are layout-dependent DOM properties. Reading them can force a synchronous style-and-layout flush if anything has dirtied layout since the last flush, once per frame, on the target where layout is most expensive. The page does write to the DOM during play — the status line about once a second and the scoreboard on Tab (`online.rs:748-768`) — so the flush is not hypothetical. The cost is almost certainly small next to the render, and it is listed here as a measurement item rather than a claim (§12).

## 7. Depth is stored every frame and read by nothing

The scene pass stores depth with `StoreOp::Store` under a comment naming stage C and later SSAO/TAA as the readers (`renderer.rs:701-704`), and the presenter's bind group contains exactly two entries — the scene *color* view and a sampler (`renderer.rs:909-922`, layout at `:461-481`). The present shader samples one texture (`present.wgsl:31`). Verified: nothing in the tree reads scene depth.

On an immediate-mode desktop GPU this is nearly free; depth already lives in memory. On the tile-based GPUs in every phone and many tablets it is not free, because on a tiler depth lives in fast on-chip tile memory and `Store` is an instruction to write it back to main memory. Discarding depth at end of pass is the standard mobile practice for exactly this reason. Derived, at 4 bytes per pixel per scene frame:

| Scene resolution | Per frame | At 60 Hz |
|---|---|---|
| 1280×720 | 3.7 MB | 221 MB/s |
| 2560×1440 | 14.7 MB | 885 MB/s |

Nearly a gigabyte per second of writeback, on the class of device least able to afford it, for data no code reads.

The argument against changing it is already written down and it is a good one: a depth buffer discarded at end of pass is an attachment configuration change, and configuration changes are where the invariant quietly dies (`docs/presenter-architecture.md:49`). That reasoning is sound and this document does not dispute it. But the reasoning weighs a maintenance risk against an unpriced cost, and the cost is the table above. Both can be had. The invariant that matters is that the depth *texture* exists, is allocated at scene-frame size, and carries `TEXTURE_BINDING` usage so it is bindable the day stage C arrives (`renderer.rs:885-894`) — none of which the store operation affects. Only the `StoreOp` in the pass descriptor changes, from `Store` to `Discard`, and tying it to a named constant that stage C flips (something on the order of `const WARP_READS_DEPTH: bool`) keeps the coupling explicit and makes the flip a one-token change reviewed in the same commit that adds the reader. That preserves the doc's actual concern — that nobody silently reconfigures the attachment and discovers it later — while not spending a phone's bandwidth on it in the meantime.

## 8. Per-frame CPU work: small, but entirely avoidable

None of this is large. It is listed because it is free to fix and because on a weak CPU running a wasm allocator the constant factor is worse than the same code natively.

**The renderer allocates and frees on the order of sixteen heap blocks per scene frame.** `vec![Vec::new(); self.meshes.len()]` allocates the outer bucket vector (`renderer.rs:644`); each mesh that receives at least one instance allocates its inner vector on first push (`:647-652`); `raws` and `ranges` are two more (`:654-655`). Derived, for a full match in which all 13 mesh buckets receive at least one instance — every gun mesh is used by the remote players and the viewmodel alike, every arm mesh by the viewmodel: 1 + 13 + 2 = **16 allocations and 16 frees per scene frame**, roughly 960 per second at 60 Hz. Every instance is also copied twice — once into its bucket (`:647`), once into `raws` (`:661`) — before the single `write_buffer` upload (`:669-670`). Retaining the buckets and `raws` across frames and clearing rather than reallocating removes all of it; the double copy can go with a counting pass, though at 132 instances the copy cost is trivial next to the allocation churn.

**The game's per-frame instance vector is created with a capacity that a full match exceeds.** `Vec::with_capacity(96)` (`online.rs:786`) against the derived 132-instance full-match figure from §5 means at least one growth reallocation and memcpy per frame once six or more players are alive. A capacity of 256 costs 4 KB and removes it. This is the smallest finding in the document and the easiest to fix.

**Small collections use hashing where arrays would do.** Each server state message rebuilds `new_from`, `self.to`, and `self.latest` as `HashMap`s and builds two more for audio diffing (`online.rs:467-476`, `:533-554`), five map allocations plus SipHash hashing of `u8` keys, at the 30 Hz state cadence implied by `STATE_EVERY_TICKS = 2` (`crates/arena-core/src/proto.rs:15`) against a 60 Hz server tick. With a hard cap of eight players (`shooter.rs:71`) a fixed array indexed by player id is strictly better on every axis. Again small — roughly 150 map allocations per second — and again free.

**What is already right, and should not be disturbed.** The instance buffer grows by powers of two and then stops (`renderer.rs:664-667`, initial capacity 64 at `:453`), so a full match reallocates it once and never again. Instances are bucketed by mesh into contiguous ranges so each mesh draws in one instanced call (`renderer.rs:642-663`, `:714-719`) — thirteen draws, as §5 counts. Audio is synthesised once on the first user gesture and cached behind a null check (`crates/arena/src/sound.rs:152-163` in the wasm module), not per play. And the hidden-tab catch-up case is explicitly handled: buffered sound cues are suppressed when more than six messages arrive at once (`online.rs:419`, `:631-637`) and `dt` is clamped so a stall does not teleport the world (`app.rs:262`). Somebody thought about the slow case in the game logic; the gap is that nobody has yet thought about it in the pixel pipeline.

## 9. The weak client's simulation: no fixed floor, and a prediction that degrades with frame rate

The brief for this assessment assumed a deterministic 60 Hz sim as a fixed CPU floor on the client. That is not what the shipped web game does, and the difference matters in both directions.

**Verified:** the fixed-step accumulator exists in local pong (`crates/arena/src/lib.rs:162-186`) and on the server (`FIXED_DT = 1.0/60.0`, `shooter.rs:5`), but `ShooterGame::update` — the shipped arena client, native and web alike — has no accumulator and no fixed step. It integrates everything with the render frame's `dt`: the time bases at `online.rs:405-409`, the zoom and stance damping at `:642` and `:659`, and the movement prediction at `:731-740`.

The good half is real: there is no fixed CPU floor. A client at 20 FPS does one-third the client-side work of a client at 60 FPS, which is exactly the right scaling for a weak device and better than a fixed-tick client would manage.

The bad half is a correctness gradient rather than a cost. The same trajectory is integrated at three different step sizes by three different pieces of code:

- the client predicts locally with the render `dt` (`online.rs:731-740`);
- the client replays unacked commands with per-command durations derived from the 0.05 s send cadence (`online.rs:566-575`, cadence at `:695`);
- the server integrates at a fixed 1/60 s (`shooter.rs:5`).

`move_circle` is not step-size-independent. It tests the destination point only, with no sweep, and resolves the two axes separately and in a fixed order — X first, then Z (`shooter.rs:205-216`). Sliding along an obstacle face therefore lands somewhere different depending on how many steps the motion was divided into. Derived consequence: **the slower the client's frame rate, the further its prediction diverges from the server's authority, and the more often the player is visibly corrected.** The correction path is already there and already tuned — rebase and replay, with a 4-unit snap threshold (`online.rs:577-587`) and a 25-per-second smoothing constant (`:742-743`) — so the failure mode is not a jump but a persistent low-grade rubber-banding that gets worse as the device gets weaker. This is the one place where weak hardware degrades *gameplay* rather than just image quality, and neither design document covers it because both are about the render path.

Tunnelling through obstacles is *not* a risk, and the reason is worth recording so nobody adds a guard that already exists. Ground sprint speed is `MOVE_SPEED × SPRINT_MULT = 2.0 × 1.6 = 3.2` units per second, while a launched sprint deliberately keeps the old `AIR_MOVE_SPEED × SPRINT_MULT = 9.0 × 1.6 = 14.4` units per second (`shooter.rs:9-14`). The latter is the maximum horizontal step: the smallest obstacle half-extent is 0.8 (`shooter.rs:105-106`), so the thinnest obstacle plus the player radius of 0.6 (`shooter.rs:11`) presents at least 2.2 units of blocking depth. Passing through in one step would need `dt > 2.2/14.4 = 0.153` s. The engine clamps `dt` to 0.1 (`app.rs:262`), giving a maximum step of 1.44 units. Derived: the clamp bounds the worst step to about 65% of the thinnest obstacle, so no single step can cross one.

That same clamp has a different consequence that is not guarded. Below 10 FPS the clamp means the client's local time advances more slowly than real time — at a 6 FPS frame gap of 0.167 s the client advances 0.1 s, so local time runs at 60% of wall clock while the server keeps ticking at 60 Hz. The client's prediction then falls behind the authority systematically rather than randomly, and every state message corrects it in the same direction. On a device weak enough to sit below 10 FPS the game does not merely look slow; it fights its own reconciliation. Ten frames per second is the point where "slow but playable" stops being true, and that threshold is worth knowing.

## 10. The web payload: what ships, what does not, and what is not being done

**A correction first, because the repository invites the opposite assumption.** `assets/models/helmet.glb` is 21 MB and `assets/textures/` holds 6.3 MB of PNGs at 512×512 and 1024×1024. None of it reaches the web build. Those assets belong to `crates/game`, the native-only textured demo, which loads them from the filesystem at runtime (`crates/game/src/main.rs:48-51`, `:70-89`) through a PNG decoder that is itself native-only (`renderer.rs:43-46`, dependency gated at `crates/ember-engine/Cargo.toml:17-22`). The shipped web game is `crates/arena`, and the only asset it embeds is the 19 KB viewmodel GLB (`crates/arena/src/online.rs:34`). Verified. Anyone sizing the web payload from the repository's asset directory will be wrong by an order of magnitude in the safe direction.

The corollary is that **the web target has no texture path at all** — `TextureData::from_png_bytes` is native-only (`renderer.rs:43-46`), so every surface in the browser game is flat instance colour through the shared 1×1 white pixel (`renderer.rs:320-324`, `shader.wgsl:66-68`). This is currently a performance advantage and a future cliff: when textures do land on the web, they will land with `mip_level_count: 1` (`renderer.rs:352`) and linear minification (`renderer.rs:316-317`) on repeat-addressed surfaces (`:314-315`). A floor tiled twelve times across the arena and minified without mipmaps is the textbook case for texture-cache thrashing and shimmer, and it is the single most expensive mistake available on a mobile GPU. No mipmap generation exists anywhere in the tree — verified, `mip_level_count: 1` at all three creation sites (`renderer.rs:352`, `:878`, `:888`) and no mip-generation code. This belongs on the list before textures reach the web build, not after.

**The build is not configured for the web at all, and this is the largest unaddressed startup cost.** Verified as an absence: the workspace manifest has a `[profile.dev]` section and no `[profile.release]` section (`Cargo.toml:37-42`), and no crate manifest defines one. The release build therefore uses cargo's defaults — `opt-level = 3`, `lto = false`, `codegen-units = 16`, `panic = "unwind"`. The deploy pipeline runs `cargo build --release` and `wasm-bindgen` and nothing else (`deploy/deploy-pages.sh:20-23`, same two commands in `README.md:176-177`); there is no `wasm-opt` pass, no size profile, and no measurement step.

For a native binary these defaults are unremarkable. For a wasm payload on a slow phone over a slow connection, download and compile time is startup latency, and each of the four defaults works against it: `codegen-units = 16` and `lto = false` block cross-crate inlining and dead-code elimination, `opt-level = 3` trades size for speed in a build where size *is* speed until the module is compiled, and `panic = "unwind"` emits unwinding machinery that a browser game does not use. A release profile with `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1`, `panic = "abort"`, and `opt-level = "s"` for the wasm target, followed by a `wasm-opt -Oz` pass, is standard practice and routinely halves a wasm binary. The size numbers are not obtainable by reading; §11's oracle and §12's commands cover it.

One structural contributor is worth naming because it is a deliberate choice with a size consequence: the wasm build enables wgpu's `webgl` feature (`crates/ember-engine/Cargo.toml:29`) on top of the default `webgpu` backend, so both backends are compiled into the module. That is the right runtime behaviour — WebGPU where available, WebGL2 fallback everywhere else, which is exactly what a weak-device story needs — and it is paid for in bytes on every download.

Two smaller payload notes. The deploy script copies the identical bundle into the arena page, the pong page, and the site root (`deploy/deploy-pages.sh:39-41`), and the crate exports both games from one library (`crates/arena/src/lib.rs:260-271`), so a player who only wants pong downloads the arena shooter's code as well. And the per-deploy cache-bust stamp (`deploy/deploy-pages.sh:53-66`, consumed at `web/games/arena/v7/index.html:180-182`) means every deploy is a full re-download for every returning player, which is correct for freshness and worth knowing as a cost on a metered connection.

**Limits are downlevelled unconditionally on wasm.** `Limits::downlevel_webgl2_defaults().using_resolution(adapter.limits())` is requested whenever the target is wasm, regardless of which backend the adapter actually is (`renderer.rs:235-239`). The comment states the intent plainly — one code path everywhere — and for today's feature set the choice costs nothing, since `using_resolution` lifts the texture-dimension limits from the real adapter so large render targets remain available. The forward-looking cost is that the WebGL2 downlevel limits are understood to zero out storage buffers and compute, which is the substrate the roadmap's GPU-driven pillar needs (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:70-71`). If that is so, the web target cannot reach the GPU-driven path even on browsers with full WebGPU, until the limits become conditional on the backend actually selected. The exact limit values are a property of the wgpu 24.0.5 limits table rather than of this repository, so this is stated as a consequence to confirm, not as a verified claim; §12 gives the check.

## 11. Prioritized levers

Ordered by expected weak-device benefit divided by cost. Each carries its evidence, what it costs, and an oracle that would show it worked.

**1. Cap the scene resolution — use `scene_scale`.** *Evidence:* the hook exists and is pinned at 1.0 with no setter (`renderer.rs:278`, `:868-869`); the backing store is DPR-multiplied and uncapped (`app.rs:233-234`), giving 3.69 M pixels on a 2× panel (§6). *Cost:* a setter, a policy for choosing the scale, and a re-bind on change — the presenter already re-creates targets and rebinds on resize (`renderer.rs:562-576`). *Oracle:* at a fixed camera pose on a 2× display, setting `scene_scale = 0.5` must reduce scene-pass GPU time by roughly 4× while the DOM crosshair and any overlay stay pixel-identical, since they composite at surface resolution.

**2. Turn on backface culling.** *Evidence:* `cull_mode: None` (`renderer.rs:963`); all shipped-game meshes are closed boxes viewed from outside, with counter-clockwise outward winding verified at `renderer.rs:1073-1080`. *Cost:* one line, plus a winding audit of `crates/game`'s `plane_mesh` (`crates/game/src/main.rs:128`) and the twelve viewmodel GLB primitives. *Oracle:* a readback of the rendered image before and after must be bit-identical for the arena scene — anything that changes is a mesh with the wrong winding, which is precisely what the audit is looking for — while rasterized-primitive count halves.

**3. Discard scene depth until a reader exists.** *Evidence:* stored at `renderer.rs:701-704`, and the present bind group holds only colour and a sampler (`renderer.rs:909-922`); 221–885 MB/s of writeback derived in §7. *Cost:* one `StoreOp`, gated on a named constant so stage C flips it in the commit that adds the reader; the texture, its size, and its `TEXTURE_BINDING` usage are untouched (`renderer.rs:885-894`), which preserves the invariant `docs/presenter-architecture.md:49` is protecting. *Oracle:* on a tile-based mobile GPU, frame time at fixed resolution drops measurably; on an immediate-mode desktop GPU it does not change — the two results together confirm the mechanism is tiler writeback rather than something else.

**4. Give the web target the throttle and the readouts.** *Evidence:* every piece of the rig is native-only (`crates/ember-engine/src/lib.rs:10-11`, `renderer.rs:539-548`, `:196-199`, `:611-612`, `app.rs:282-302`), contradicting the adopted document's stated purpose for it (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:120-122`). *Cost:* the throttle itself is target-independent arithmetic on `web_time::Instant`, which the engine already uses on both targets (`app.rs:3`); the readout needs no egui, since the page has a DOM and already writes to it (`online.rs:155-167`) — and the readout half should follow `docs/latency-observability.md:205` rather than inventing a second surface, leaving only the throttle control as new work here. *Oracle:* the scene-Hz cap set from the page visibly holds the scene rate while the presenter continues at rAF rate, and the reported scene age rises to match the cap — which is the same behaviour the native rig already demonstrates, now observable where it matters.

**5. Configure the release profile and add a size pass.** *Evidence:* no `[profile.release]` anywhere (`Cargo.toml:37-42`); the pipeline is two commands with no size step (`deploy/deploy-pages.sh:20-23`). *Cost:* a manifest section and one command in the deploy script; `lto = "fat"` and `codegen-units = 1` cost build wall time, which is a server-lane concern rather than a player-facing one. *Oracle:* the `.wasm` byte size before and after, plus time-to-first-frame on a throttled connection; the profile change is worth keeping only if the size drop is large enough to matter, which is why the measurement precedes the decision.

**6. Retain the per-frame scratch buffers, and size the instance vector correctly.** *Evidence:* sixteen allocations and frees per scene frame (`renderer.rs:644-655`); `Vec::with_capacity(96)` against a derived 132-instance full match (`online.rs:786`, §5). *Cost:* buckets and `raws` become renderer fields cleared each frame; one constant changes. *Oracle:* allocation count per frame in a wasm heap profile goes to zero for the render path — a count, not a timing, which is why it is checkable without a stable benchmark.

**7. Add frustum culling before stage B, not after.** *Evidence:* no culling exists anywhere in the workspace (§5), while the adopted document's guard-band requirement presumes it does (`docs/atw-first-rendering.md:105-106`). *Cost:* real work, unlike items 1–6 — a bounding volume per instance and a plane test — which is why it ranks below the one-liners despite mattering more once the guard band lands. *Oracle:* instance count submitted per frame drops by roughly half with the camera at the arena centre, and by construction the rendered image is unchanged.

**8. Mipmaps, before textures reach the web build.** *Evidence:* `mip_level_count: 1` at every creation site with linear minification on repeat-addressed surfaces (`renderer.rs:352`, `:314-317`); no generation code exists. *Cost:* a mip chain and a generation pass; deferrable exactly as long as the web build has no textures (`renderer.rs:43-46`). *Oracle:* on a tiled floor viewed at a grazing angle, texture-cache miss rate and shimmer both fall; the visual half of this one is genuinely checkable by eye, unlike most of the list.

## 12. Measurement items

None of these can be settled by reading, and none was run for this document. Each names what a server lane would run.

**M1 — wasm payload size, as shipped and under a size profile.** `cargo build --target wasm32-unknown-unknown --release -p arena --lib && wasm-bindgen --target web --no-typescript --out-dir web/pkg target/wasm32-unknown-unknown/release/arena.wasm && ls -l web/pkg/arena_bg.wasm`; then again with a `[profile.release]` carrying `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1`, `panic = "abort"`, `opt-level = "s"`; then again after `wasm-opt -Oz`. Lever 5 follows from the spread of the three numbers.

**M2 — payload composition.** `twiggy top -n 40 web/pkg/arena_bg.wasm` and `twiggy dominators web/pkg/arena_bg.wasm`: how much of the module is the two wgpu backends (`crates/ember-engine/Cargo.toml:29`), how much is `serde_json`, how much is the game.

**M3 — the WebGL2 downlevel limits table.** Whether `Limits::downlevel_webgl2_defaults()` in wgpu 24.0.5 zeroes `max_storage_buffers_per_shader_stage` and `max_compute_workgroup_size_x` — `cargo doc -p wgpu`, or the limits table in the registry source. Settles §10's conditional claim about the GPU-driven pillar on the web.

**M4 — the ATW tax, measured rather than derived.** Present-pass GPU time at 1280×720 and 2560×1440 on an integrated GPU and on a phone, via `timestamp_writes` on the present pass descriptor (currently `None`, `renderer.rs:744`) where supported, or A/B against a build drawing the scene straight to the swapchain. This is the figure `docs/atw-first-rendering.md:126` quotes and §2 derives as bandwidth.

**M5 — depth store on a tiler.** Frame time with `StoreOp::Store` versus `StoreOp::Discard` on the scene depth attachment (`renderer.rs:703`), phone and desktop, at fixed resolution.

**M6 — backface culling.** Rasterized-primitive count and frame time at `renderer.rs:963` with `None` versus `Some(wgpu::Face::Back)`, plus a readback image comparison to catch winding errors.

**M7 — the per-frame layout read.** Whether `client_width()`/`client_height()` at `app.rs:233-234` forces a synchronous layout flush during play: a browser performance profile over an active match, looking for layout entries inside the rAF callback.

**M8 — prediction divergence against frame rate.** With the client throttled to 60, 30, 20 and 10 FPS, the distribution of the rebase correction magnitude at `online.rs:576-587` over a fixed scripted movement against a real server. The shape of the growth determines whether a fixed-step client accumulator is worth adding.

**M9 — the actual weak-device baseline.** Time-to-first-frame and sustained frame rate for the live page on a real low-end phone and a real integrated-GPU laptop, at a known canvas size, with and without lever 1. Every other item here is a component; this is the one that answers the title question.
